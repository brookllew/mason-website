Edward Sexton Google Ads and Meta: What Changed in 2026

Meta from January 2025 and Google Ads from its launch in May 2026, through Nov. 15, 2026. Meta figures are in pounds and Google Ads figures in dollars; combined figures convert Google at $1.34 to the pound, the rate the Edward Sexton store uses. Figures for 2026 leave out two large orders Meta recorded that Edward Sexton has told us did not come from the ads: £18,900 in June and £7,007 in October. Prepared by Mason Interactive for Edward Sexton.

Paid media is working harder for Edward Sexton than it did a year ago. Meta spend came down from about £8,300 a month (monthly average) in July 2025 to January 2026 to about £5,000 from February, and on March 31 Edward Sexton set paid media at £5,000 to £6,000 a month across all channels. Recorded purchases fell with the budget, from about 23 a month to about 15. Each pound now goes further: cost per purchase improved from £361 to £317, return on ad spend rose from 2.4 to 2.8, and purchase value fell 32% on 43% less spend. Google Ads, launched in May, adds about five purchases a month, and recorded its best months in August and September. October was softer on both platforms, and the Google part of that is mostly fixable inside brand search.

What we recommend: plan budget around sales, when Edward Sexton's customers buy; tighten Google brand search, where most Google purchases come from; rebuild the Meta remarketing audience, then give it a steadier share of the monthly budget; and agree a new-customer measure with Edward Sexton and its ecommerce agency, using Shopify orders, so every channel is judged the same way. The volume plan follows directly below; the full list is at the end, under Next Steps.

The budget explains the volume. Clicks fell almost exactly in line with spend, and the share of clicks that became purchases did not move. Nothing in the account's efficiency explains the lower purchase count.

Return improved more than cost per purchase. That is the larger orders (Further Down the Funnel), which also makes return on ad spend sensitive to one or two big purchases a month.

Thesis II: Remarketing Is the Lowest-Cost Source of Purchases

Remarketing converts at about half the cost of acquisition. It recorded 39 purchases from April to October 2026 at £195 each, against £442 for acquisition.

Its audience shrank after the sale, not just its budget. It ran at £580 to £610 a month in September and October, and its ads were shown about 15,000 times a month, against about 140,000 in August. The ad set Mason launched on Sept. 2 targets only past purchasers and people who added to cart, narrowed by location and income, and reached about 200 to 250 people a month, each about 65 times. The versions that ran from January to August reached 16,000 to 35,000 people a month. Until site visitors and Facebook and Instagram engagers are added back, more budget would only raise how often the same few people see the ads.

It reaches people who already know the brand. That makes it the cheapest way to close a sale, and not a replacement for the new-customer work Edward Sexton's ecommerce agency has asked for. Acquisition, Awareness and The Trade Desk build the audience that remarketing then converts, so the mix matters more than any one line.

Sale months buy volume at a good price. The 2025 sales delivered purchases at £146 to £199 each. August 2026 delivered 27 Meta purchases and Google's best month, at about the usual cost per purchase.

The January 2026 sale was softer. It recorded a third of January 2025's purchases on similar spend. Mason's review of that sale found the remarketing ads drew far fewer clicks than the year before, so the creative is the first place to look for the winter sale.

So the calendar is the biggest lever. Concentrating budget in the first week of each sale, as Edward Sexton has done historically, is likely to add more purchases than spreading the same money evenly.

Brand search is the foundation it was meant to be. Every purchase that can be traced to a search came from a search that included the brand name, at a return of 4.4 on that spend.

Non-brand searches have not converted yet. They are worth reaching for new customers, but they need their own budget and goal, not a share of brand search. The accompanying key term gaps workbook separates the two.

Google reports the search terms behind about half of spend; the rest of Performance Max runs on placements without a search term.

Thesis V: Performance Max Pays Back When It Is Focused

Performance Max spend (bars, left axis) and purchases (line, right axis), per month.

The first two months were learning. In May and June it bought 18,400 clicks at about 15 cents each, largely on display placements, and recorded 1 purchase.

Focused, it worked. After it was narrowed to top products and the right locations in late July, it recorded 17 purchases worth $12,342 on $3,941 from July to September, a return of 3.1.

October and November are quiet. It spent $816 with no purchases after budgets were moved toward brand search. A new autumn and winter asset group went in during October and November.

Thesis VI: Brand Search Needs Tightening After the Summer Changes

National brand search click-through rate (left axis) and cost per click (right axis), per month.

The campaign drifted wider. Click-through rate fell from 29% in June and 27% in July to 17% in August and September and 5% in October, while cost per click rose from about $1.30 to $4.26. That is the pattern of a brand campaign serving on searches that are not about the brand.

Two changes line up with it. Broad-match keywords were added in late July; one of them, "edward london clothing," spent $1,077 with no purchase and was paused on Nov. 16. On Sept. 24 the strongest brand searches in New York and Los Angeles moved to their own campaign, which recorded 2.3 purchases in October while the national campaign recorded none.

What comes next. Add the non-brand negatives in the key term gaps workbook, and either exclude New York and Los Angeles from the national campaign or fold the two back together.

Thesis VIII: The Search Ads Can Ask for the Sale

The main brand ad, May 1 to Nov. 15, 2026.

Strong on brand, light on action. The main brand ad carries 14 headlines and 4 descriptions about the house and its heritage. None invites the reader to shop, book a fitting or see a price, and none mentions a sale, delivery or returns. "Edward Sexton Official" is the headline behind most of its purchases.

Two landing pages to fix. Two trouser ads, one in each brand campaign, send people to the jackets page.

The accompanying ad copy workbook has rewritten copy for each ad group, keeping the brand voice and adding a clear next step.

Thesis IX: Measurement Is Ready for a New-Customer Goal

Google purchase counts are clean. Google counts one action as a purchase, recorded through the store's Google channel. A secondary "view item" action began passing product prices in August, which inflates the "all conversions value" column into the millions; it does not touch purchase counts, and reports use purchases only.

Meta counts a purchase if someone clicked in the past 7 days or saw an ad the day before, so some purchases also appear in Google, and neither platform separates new from returning customers. It can also claim orders the ads did not drive: the two large orders left out of this report were both recorded by Meta.

Shopify settles both. Orders by source and by new or returning customer, monthly, would let Edward Sexton, its ecommerce agency and Mason judge every channel, including The Trade Desk, on one number.

A quiet first half, with the new creative only just live. The November refresh and gifting ads went live on Nov. 12 and 13, so most of this period ran on older creative.
