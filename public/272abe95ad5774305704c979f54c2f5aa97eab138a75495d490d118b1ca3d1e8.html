The Community Solution: The Rewrites Are Working

Prepared November 24, 2026 · Accipiter snapshot November 23, 2026 · Updates the November 5 review, same method, same baselines

Four months in, the same 1,215 Google Ads keywords that started with a Below average landing page experience rating improved from 2.17 to 2.52 in average Quality Score (paired test, p < 0.000001). The rollout timing points to the rewrites: once the later batch went live, its share of keywords moving out of Below average rose by 9.2 percentage points, compared with 3.6 points for the earlier batch over the same period.

At a glance

The problem. The Community Solution spends heavily on paid search across six schools. Google rates every keyword on how well the page it lands on answers that particular query — its landing page experience rating. That rating, along with expected click-through rate and ad relevance, is part of the ad quality Google assesses at auction time, and it is one of the few levers a rewrite can actually pull. Google also publishes a 1–10 Quality Score per keyword, summarising those same components, and that is the number tracked throughout this report. In July, two thirds of the portfolio’s scored keywords were rated below average on landing page experience — Google’s way of saying the page did not answer the search that reached it.

What we did. Mason’s AI audit tool, Accipiter, read all 76 landing pages against the queries actually reaching each one and wrote 243 specific copy recommendations — headline, subhead, fast facts, calls to action — each with the reasoning and the exact wording to use.

What The Community Solution did. The team published those recommendations across 72 pages between September 9 and October 23, in waves, reworking the ones that needed reworking for program accuracy along the way.

How we are measuring it. Two ways, independently. Accipiter crawls every page weekly and records its Quality Score, so each page is compared against its own score in the week before its first edit. Separately, Google’s own per-keyword ratings are matched before and after on keyword and ad group, so the same search terms are followed through the whole period. Keywords added along the way are excluded from that second measure, which is what keeps it clean.

The timing points to the rewrites

The October review compared two groups of pages: those whose rewrites were already live, and those still waiting. The first group was pulling search terms out of below average more than twice as often as the second. The obvious objection to any comparison like that is that the two groups were different pages to begin with, and perhaps they were always going to behave differently.

That objection no longer applies, because the waiting group has since been rewritten too — the October 15 and October 23 batches. We can now watch the same pages before and after their own rewrite.

The group that was behind caught up once it got the same treatment. It gained 9.2 points over the six weeks in which its rewrites went live. The group that had already been rewritten gained 3.6 points over the same six weeks, and the gap between them closed from eight and a half points to three.

What this does and does not rule out. Comparing each group with its own earlier self removes any fixed difference between the two sets of pages — that was the main objection to the October comparison. It does not remove things that changed over time. Performance Max and the ad copy refresh went live in late October, and the November keyword window (October 25 – November 23) sits entirely after them; the October measurement predates them. The reason we still read the timing as evidence for the rewrites is that both groups lived through those same changes and only the newly rewritten one moved sharply.

Every term in this test carries a Google rating from both July–August and November, matched on keyword and ad group. Terms added since August have no earlier rating and are excluded entirely, so none of this can be an artefact of new keywords being added. Matching on ad group as well as keyword means a term moved into a different ad group drops out rather than appearing as an improvement.

Tying each keyword to the page it lands on relies on a click-weighted map from ad group to landing page, and the size of the remaining gap depends on how strictly that map is filtered: with no filter it is +7.5 points (p = 0.003), at 60% confidence +3.0 (p = 0.25), and at 90% confidence +0.9 (p = 0.77). The gap narrows as the mapping gets more reliable, which suggests the larger figures carry mis-assigned terms — but we chose the threshold after seeing the results, so we are showing all of them. What holds at every threshold is the late group rising from 6.0% to between 15% and 17%.

How each school moved

Everything above is measured on individual search terms, because that is where Google publishes its judgement. This section switches to whole pages — the Quality Score view we have reported since October, and the one most people picture when they hear the phrase.

It is a blunter instrument. A page’s Quality Score is an average across every keyword landing on it, so a handful of terms improving barely moves it, and adding or cutting keywords moves it whether or not the page changed. Both views agree that the portfolio improved. They do not line up school by school, and that is worth knowing before reading the chart.

Fidelity findings

The November review reported a pattern where pages that kept more of the drafted wording improved more, flagged it as a lead rather than a finding, and promised a recheck once the October edits had three weeks behind them. The recheck has been run, and it needed a better measuring stick than the one we had been using.

Measured against page-level Quality Score, the pattern still looks like nothing. A page’s Quality Score averages across every keyword landing on it — including terms that were already fine and terms the edit never set out to address — and that drowns the signal. Measured against the thing the edits were actually aimed at, the share of a page’s below average terms that climbed out, it is unmistakable.

Pages that went live close to as drafted pulled twice as many of their below-average terms up a grade. 46 pages, each weighted equally, so these are the average per-page rates rather than a pooled count of terms. Pooling the 925 terms instead gives 29.4% against 12.5% — same picture, slightly smaller gap. The correlation between how much of the copy survived and how many terms climbed is 0.46.

Read this as an association, not a mechanism. It is the strongest page-level result in the project and it survived both checks below, but it emerged only once we changed the outcome measure, and nothing here establishes that the surviving wording is what caused the improvement rather than something that travels with it.

Two checks, because a result that only appears on the fourth attempt deserves them:

  • It is not just Saybrook. The split holds inside The Chicago School on its own — 27.8% across 11 high-fidelity pages against 15.1% across 12 low-fidelity ones. Inside Saybrook it is 51.1% against 32.2%. With school removed entirely, the correlation is 0.42 (p = 0.004).
  • It is not an artefact of tying keywords to pages. The result is stable however strictly that mapping is filtered: p between 0.002 and 0.005, correlation between 0.43 and 0.50, slightly stronger at the most reliable mapping.
What this does not explain. Because the pattern holds within each school, it is not the reason Saybrook moved more than Chicago. Both schools have high-fidelity and low-fidelity pages, and in both the high-fidelity ones did better. The gap between the schools remains unexplained.

Why the schools look different

Saybrook’s number is far ahead of The Chicago School’s, and most of that gap is keyword mix rather than rewrite performance. Every school’s existing search terms improved. What separates the headline numbers is largely what each school added while the rewrites were going in.

The Chicago School’s existing terms improved by 9.5 points, from 59.8% below average to 50.3%. It then added 669 new search terms in pursuit of growth, and those are rated below average 62.9% of the time — worse than the book they joined. That is not a fault; broad new terms start below average almost by definition, because they are reaching for queries the pages were never built to answer. But it means every aggregate number for Chicago is averaging improved old terms against unproven new ones, and understates what the rewrites did.

How much of the gap this accounts for. On all scored terms, Chicago sits 26.5 points behind Saybrook on the share rated below average. Comparing only the terms both schools held in August — keyword mix held fixed — that gap narrows to 5.7 points. So most of the difference is mix, but not all of it: Saybrook’s existing terms also improved about twice as far as Chicago’s, 17.4 points against 9.5. One caveat on reading the two tables together — this one counts landing-page-experience ratings, while the school chart earlier measures page-level Quality Score. They are related but not the same number.

Saybrook is the mirror image. It added 419 terms rated below average only 17.9% of the time — tightly matched to pages that had just been rewritten to serve them. Its existing terms improved 17.4 points and its new terms arrived already in good shape, so both halves of its number pull the same way.

Four other explanations for the school gap were tested and did not survive: how much of the drafted copy carried through (it holds within each school, so it cannot explain differences between them), the number of edits made to a page, how long a page has been live since its edit, and how low the page started. Ad group restructuring also fails: Saybrook rebuilt into 1,090 new ad groups and Chicago into 15, but UWS made one and The Colleges of Law none, and both gained.

A note on measurement

Quality Score has three parts, and only one of them — landing page experience — is in anyone’s control when they edit a page. Google publishes that rating per keyword, as Below average, Average or Above average. So rather than averaging pages, we followed individual search terms.

For the October reading, 1,756 terms were rated by Google both before the rewrites (July 20 – August 18) and after (October 12). The same exercise has now been repeated against the October 25 – November 23 window, giving 1,857 matched terms across all six schools, of which 1,215 started at Below average.

What we know, and what we are still watching

  • The rewrites work, and we have now tested it two ways. Across groups in October: 14.5% against 6.0% (χ², p = 0.000022). Within the same group before and after its own rewrite: 6.0% rising to 15.2%. The second is the harder one to explain away.
  • More of the stuck terms are coming unstuck. Of the terms Google rated below average in August, 11.4% had climbed to a better rating by mid-October. By November 23 that was 18.8%. These are two separate matched sets rather than one cohort followed through three dates, so the share is comparable but the counts are not subtractable. A separate measure, Accipiter’s landing-page-experience share, fell from 56.6% to 49.7% over the same period (p = 0.0019).
  • How closely the draft carried through matters. Pages where half or more of the drafted wording survived pulled twice as many below-average terms up a grade — 32.4% against 15.0%, p = 0.002 — and the split holds inside The Chicago School on its own. Measured on page Quality Score it looked like nothing; measured on the terms the edits were aimed at, it is the strongest page-level result we have.
  • Saybrook has now replicated. Ten pages became twenty and the average held at +0.53 (p = 0.0003). UWS shows the same effect independently at +0.42 (p = 0.003).
  • The gains hold over time. Pages already up at the start of November are up slightly more now, not less.
  • The gap between schools is a growth effect, not a rewrite effect. Every school’s existing terms improved. The Chicago School added 669 new terms rated below average 62.9% of the time; Saybrook added 419 at 17.9%. Aggregate numbers mix the two, which is why Chicago’s look flat.
  • No single page can be read on its own. Week-to-week movement on one page is routinely 0.3 or more with no changes at all, so every row in the appendix is one noisy observation rather than a verdict on that page.
  • Other changes are now in the mix. Performance Max and the ad copy refresh at The Chicago School and Saybrook went live in late October, and both move Quality Score independently of the pages. The keyword result above predates them; the page-level averages from here on include them.

What we do next, together

  • Put the Saybrook and Chicago edits side by side for an hour. This is the highest-value thing either team can do this month. Two schools, same method, opposite results, and the numbers have told us everything they are going to tell us on their own.
  • Pick the first of the six new pages. By spend the order is MFT, Psy.D., BCBA, licensed therapist, sport psychology, teaching credential. The MFT page alone sits under $63K of thirty-day spend at an average Quality Score of 2.2.
  • Refresh the keyword-level read with the December pull. That is the measurement that carries the main finding, and it is due.
  • Tidy ten rows in the tracker. Nine recommendations carry a status rather than a go-live date, and one page points at a replacement URL. One page in the edit log, Psy.D. School Psychology, is no longer tracked by Accipiter at all and should be added back.
